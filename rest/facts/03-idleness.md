# Facts: Idleness and Leisure

### Otium and negotium
- **Fact:** Latin *negotium* (business) is formed from *nec* + *otium*, literally "not-leisure."
- **Source:** Standard Latin etymology (e.g. Lewis & Short, s.v. negotium)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** rome, latin, etymology, work

### Ascholia and schole
- **Fact:** Greek *ascholia* (business, lack of leisure) is the negation of *scholē* (leisure), and *scholē* is the root of Latin *schola* and English "school."
- **Source:** Liddell-Scott-Jones; standard English etymological dictionaries
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** greece, etymology, leisure, school

### Aristotle on busyness for leisure
- **Fact:** Aristotle wrote that we are busy in order to have leisure, as we make war in order to live in peace.
- **Source:** Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 10.7 (1177b4-6); cf. Politics 7.14
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** aristotle, greece, philosophy, leisure

### Scipio never less idle
- **Fact:** Cicero reports that Scipio Africanus (as told by Cato) used to say he was never less idle than when at leisure, nor less alone than when alone; Cicero contrasts this with his own unwilling leisure under the political circumstances of the time.
- **Source:** Cicero, De Officiis 3.1-2 (44 BC)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** rome, cicero, otium, politics

### Cum dignitate otium
- **Fact:** Cicero used the phrase *cum dignitate otium*, "leisure with dignity," as a political ideal. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Cicero, Pro Sestio 98
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** rome, cicero, otium, politics

### Siesta from sexta hora
- **Fact:** Spanish *siesta* derives from Latin *sexta (hora)*, the sixth hour counted from sunrise, i.e. about noon.
- **Source:** Standard etymology (RAE; OED)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** siesta, spain, rome, etymology, time

### Benedict's noon rest
- **Fact:** The Rule of Saint Benedict allows monks, after the sixth-hour meal, to rest on their beds in silence; anyone who wishes to read must read to himself so as not to disturb others.
- **Source:** Rule of Saint Benedict, chapter 48 (6th century)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** monasticism, benedict, siesta, medieval, reading

### Noonday demon in the Psalms
- **Fact:** The Septuagint text of Psalm 90 (Hebrew 91), verse 6, speaks of the *daimonion mesembrinon*, "the noonday demon"; the Vulgate has *daemonio meridiano*.
- **Source:** Septuagint Psalm 90:6; Vulgate Psalm 90:6
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** bible, acedia, demons, noon

### Evagrius on acedia
- **Fact:** Evagrius Ponticus listed acedia among the eight evil thoughts and described it attacking the monk from about the fourth to the eighth hour, making the sun seem not to move and the day seem fifty hours long, driving him to the window and out of his cell to watch for the ninth hour and for visiting brothers.
- **Source:** Evagrius Ponticus, Praktikos 12 (late 4th century)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** acedia, monasticism, egypt, desert, time

### Cassian brings acedia west
- **Fact:** John Cassian transmitted the Evagrian scheme of eight vices to the Latin West, devoting book 10 of his Institutes to acedia.
- **Source:** John Cassian, De institutis coenobiorum, book 10 (c. 420s)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** acedia, monasticism, gaul, vices

### Gregory folds acedia into sadness
- **Fact:** Gregory the Great's list of seven vices merged acedia with *tristitia* (sadness); later medieval lists restored acedia, which became "sloth."
- **Source:** Gregory the Great, Moralia in Job 31.45; see Siegfried Wenzel, The Sin of Sloth (1967)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** acedia, sloth, vices, medieval

### The sloth named for the sin
- **Fact:** The mammal "sloth" takes its English name from the vice, as does Portuguese *preguiça* (laziness).
- **Source:** OED, s.v. sloth
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** sloth, animals, etymology, americas

### Seneca on the Sabbath
- **Fact:** Augustine quotes Seneca as criticizing the Jews for losing almost a seventh of their lives in idleness by keeping the Sabbath.
- **Source:** Augustine, City of God 6.11 (quoting a lost work of Seneca, De superstitione)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** sabbath, seneca, augustine, rome, week

### The Republican décade
- **Fact:** The French Republican Calendar (in use 1793-1805) replaced the seven-day week with the ten-day décade, with rest on the tenth day (décadi).
- **Source:** Decree of the National Convention, October 1793; Gregorian calendar restored 1 January 1806
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** france, revolution, calendar, week

### The Soviet nepreryvka
- **Fact:** From 1929 the Soviet Union ran a continuous five-day week with staggered days off by worker group (reportedly color-coded), so families could have different rest days; it gave way to a six-day week from 1931 and the seven-day week returned in 1940.
- **Source:** Eviatar Zerubavel, The Seven Day Circle (1985); the color detail is widely reported but I have not verified a primary source
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** soviet-union, calendar, week, labor

### Land of Cokaygne poem
- **Fact:** The Middle English poem "The Land of Cokaygne" (early 14th century, in British Library MS Harley 913) describes houses built of food and roast geese flying about crying "Geese, all hot!"
- **Source:** The Land of Cokaygne, BL MS Harley 913
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** cockaigne, medieval, utopia, food, england

### Paid to sleep in Cockaigne
- **Fact:** The Old French "Fabliau de Cocagne" (13th century) says that in Cockaigne the more one sleeps the more one earns, and that sleeping until noon earns five and a half sous.
- **Source:** Li fabliaus de Coquaigne; discussed in Herman Pleij, Dreaming of Cockaigne (Dutch 1997; English 2001). Exact sum recalled, not rechecked against the text.
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** cockaigne, france, fabliau, sleep, wages

### Bruegel's porridge mountain
- **Fact:** In the Dutch tradition one enters Luilekkerland (Cockaigne) by eating through a mountain of porridge; Pieter Bruegel the Elder's "The Land of Cockaigne" (1567) shows a man emerging from it.
- **Source:** Bruegel, Het Luilekkerland, 1567, Alte Pinakothek, Munich
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** cockaigne, bruegel, netherlands, painting

### Saint Monday
- **Fact:** In 18th- and 19th-century England, artisans paid by the piece commonly took Monday off ("Saint Monday") and made up the work later in the week.
- **Source:** E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline and Industrial Capitalism," Past & Present 38 (1967); Douglas Reid, "The Decline of Saint Monday 1766-1876," Past & Present 71 (1976)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** saint-monday, england, labor, industrial-revolution

### Franklin never made a Saint Monday
- **Fact:** Benjamin Franklin wrote that while working at Watts's printing house in London (c. 1725) he never made a Saint Monday, which recommended him to his master.
- **Source:** Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, part 1
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** franklin, saint-monday, printing, london

### Blauer Montag
- **Fact:** The German equivalent custom was "blauer Montag," Blue Monday.
- **Source:** Standard German usage; discussed in Thompson (1967)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** germany, saint-monday, labor, idiom

### Robert Owen's three eights
- **Fact:** In 1817 Robert Owen formulated the slogan "Eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest."
- **Source:** Commonly dated to 1817 in labor histories
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** owen, eight-hours, labor, britain

### Melbourne stonemasons 1856
- **Fact:** On 21 April 1856, Melbourne stonemasons marched off building sites and secured an eight-hour day, among the first such gains in the world.
- **Source:** Australian labor history; commemorated by the Eight Hours Day Monument, Melbourne
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** australia, eight-hours, labor, strikes

### "Eight Hours" song
- **Fact:** The American song "Eight Hours" (1878; lyrics I. G. Blanchard, music Rev. Jesse H. Jones) has the refrain "Eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will."
- **Source:** Song sheet, 1878
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** eight-hours, song, labor, united-states

### Haymarket and May Day
- **Fact:** The 1 May 1886 general strike for the eight-hour day in the US led to the Haymarket affair in Chicago, which is the origin of International Workers' Day. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Standard US labor history
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** haymarket, may-day, eight-hours, chicago

### Lafargue's Right to Be Lazy
- **Fact:** Paul Lafargue's "Le Droit à la paresse" appeared in L'Égalité in 1880 (as a pamphlet in 1883), subtitled a refutation of the "right to work" of 1848; it proposed a working day of no more than three hours.
- **Source:** Paul Lafargue, Le Droit à la paresse
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** lafargue, socialism, france, work

### Lafargue biography
- **Fact:** Lafargue was born in Santiago de Cuba (1842), trained in medicine, and married Laura Marx; in November 1911 the couple died by suicide together at Draveil, he having resolved not to live past seventy; Lenin spoke at the funeral.
- **Source:** Standard biographies of Lafargue; Lenin's funeral speech (Collected Works vol. 17)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** lafargue, marx, biography, death

### Keynes's fifteen-hour week
- **Fact:** Keynes predicted that within a hundred years three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week might suffice.
- **Source:** J. M. Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** keynes, economics, work, prediction

### Russell's four-hour day
- **Fact:** Bertrand Russell argued for a four-hour working day.
- **Source:** Bertrand Russell, "In Praise of Idleness," Harper's Magazine, 1932
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** russell, philosophy, work, idleness

### Veblen's conspicuous leisure
- **Fact:** Thorstein Veblen coined "conspicuous leisure" for the non-productive use of time as a display of status. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Thorstein Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** veblen, economics, status, leisure

### Wu wei in the Daodejing
- **Fact:** Daodejing chapter 37 says the Way constantly does nothing (wu wei), yet nothing is left undone.
- **Source:** Daodejing, ch. 37
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** daoism, wu-wei, china, philosophy

### Zhuangzi's useless oak
- **Fact:** In the Zhuangzi, carpenter Shi ignores a giant shrine tree as worthless timber; the tree appears in his dream saying it has long tried to be useless and has finally succeeded.
- **Source:** Zhuangzi, ch. 4 ("In the World of Men")
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** zhuangzi, daoism, uselessness, trees

### Huizi's crooked tree
- **Fact:** Huizi complains that his great tree is too gnarled for a measuring line and too crooked for compass or square; Zhuangzi tells him to plant it in "Not-Even-Anything Village," do nothing beside it, and sleep under it.
- **Source:** Zhuangzi, ch. 1 ("Free and Easy Wandering"), Burton Watson translation
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** zhuangzi, daoism, trees, sleep

### Lying flat
- **Fact:** In April 2021 Luo Huazhong posted "Lying Flat Is Justice" on Baidu Tieba, describing quitting work, cycling from Sichuan to Tibet, and living on about 200 yuan a month; the post and related groups were removed by censors.
- **Source:** Contemporary reporting (e.g. New York Times, BBC, mid-2021)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** tang-ping, china, internet, work, censorship

### Xi on lying flat
- **Fact:** A speech by Xi Jinping published in Qiushi in October 2021 warned against "involution" and "lying flat."
- **Source:** Qiushi, October 2021 (on common prosperity); reported widely
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** china, xi-jinping, tang-ping, politics

### Inemuri
- **Fact:** Japanese *inemuri*, roughly "being present while sleeping," is socially tolerated napping in meetings or on trains, sometimes read as a sign of diligence.
- **Source:** Brigitte Steger, Inemuri: Wie die Japaner schlafen und was wir von ihnen lernen können (2007) and related articles
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** japan, inemuri, sleep, work

### Oblomov in bed
- **Fact:** In Goncharov's Oblomov (1859), the hero does not get out of bed through most of Part One.
- **Source:** Ivan Goncharov, Oblomov (1859)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** oblomov, russia, novel, idleness

### Oblomov's Dream
- **Fact:** The chapter "Oblomov's Dream," published separately in 1849, depicts his childhood estate, where the entire household falls into an afternoon sleep after dinner while the boy slips away.
- **Source:** Goncharov, Oblomov, Part One, ch. 9
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** oblomov, russia, siesta, childhood

### Dobrolyubov's Oblomovism
- **Fact:** Nikolai Dobrolyubov's 1859 essay "What Is Oblomovism?" made the hero a type of the Russian gentry's inertia.
- **Source:** N. A. Dobrolyubov, "Chto takoe oblomovshchina?" (1859)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** oblomov, russia, criticism, idleness

### Lenin on the Oblomovs
- **Fact:** Lenin said in 1922 that Russia had made three revolutions and yet the Oblomovs remained, applying the term to Soviet bureaucrats.
- **Source:** Lenin, speech to the Communist group of the All-Russia Congress of Metalworkers, 6 March 1922
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** lenin, oblomov, bureaucracy, soviet-union

### Columbus and the hammock
- **Fact:** Columbus's journal of the first voyage, in Las Casas's abstract, describes Taíno beds like cotton nets (entry for 17 October 1492); the Taíno word *hamaca* entered Spanish and later English as "hammock."
- **Source:** Columbus, Diario (Las Casas abstract), 17 Oct. 1492; OED s.v. hammock
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** hammock, taino, columbus, caribbean, etymology

### Hammocks in the navy
- **Fact:** European navies adopted the hammock for sailors' sleeping in the late 16th century. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** General naval histories; exact date of Royal Navy adoption not verified
- **Status:** disputed
- **Tags:** hammock, navy, sailors, sleep

### Diogenes and Alexander
- **Fact:** Diogenes, lying in the sun, is said to have told Alexander the Great, who offered him anything, to stand out of his light. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Diogenes Laertius, Lives 6.38; Plutarch, Life of Alexander 14
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** diogenes, greece, cynics, sun

### Stevenson's Apology for Idlers
- **Fact:** Robert Louis Stevenson published "An Apology for Idlers" in 1877. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Cornhill Magazine, 1877; collected in Virginibus Puerisque (1881)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** stevenson, essay, idleness, victorian

### Johnson's Idler
- **Fact:** Samuel Johnson wrote the "Idler" essays in the Universal Chronicle, 1758-1760. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** The Idler, 1758-1760
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** johnson, essay, idleness, eighteenth-century

### Pieper on leisure
- **Fact:** Josef Pieper argued that leisure, not work, is the foundation of culture. (Not used in essay.)
- **Source:** Josef Pieper, Musse und Kult (1948), translated as Leisure: The Basis of Culture
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** pieper, philosophy, leisure, culture
