# The Fermata

In musical notation a whole rest hangs from the line, and a half rest sits on it. Students are told that the whole rest is heavy and the half rest is light, and this is how to remember them.

A rest of many bars is written as a single thick line with a number over it. The number says how long to do nothing.

In Italian a *fermata* is a bus stop.

Over a note or a rest the fermata is a dot under an arc, like a closed eye. It means: hold this longer than written. How much longer is not written.

Beethoven's Fifth Symphony begins with eight notes, and two of them are held under fermatas.

In an orchestral score the letters G.P. stand for *Generalpause*. Every player rests at once. In Haydn's "Farewell" Symphony, the players stop one by one, snuff their candles, and leave, until two violins are left.

On the evening of August 29, 1952, at the Maverick Concert Hall near Woodstock, New York, the pianist David Tudor sat at a piano, closed the lid, and opened it again thirty seconds later. He closed it again for two minutes and twenty-three seconds, and again for one minute and forty seconds. The hall was a barn with the back wall open to the woods. In the first movement, John Cage said afterward, you could hear the wind in the trees. In the second, rain began on the roof. In the third, the audience made interesting sounds of its own, and some of it walked out.

He said that he had gone into an anechoic chamber at Harvard, a room built to have no echo, expecting to hear nothing, and had heard two sounds, one high and one low. The engineer told him the high one was his nervous system and the low one was his blood. Engineers since have said the high one was more likely his ears.

The published score of the piece gives each movement one word: *Tacet*. It is silent.

According to Newton, every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed upon it. The Latin is *in statu suo quiescendi*. Rest and uniform motion are, for the law, the same state. A body at rest is a body going nowhere at a constant speed.

In the Talmud, in the tractate on fasts, Honi the Circle-Drawer was walking on the road and saw a man planting a carob tree. He asked how long until the tree bore fruit. Seventy years, the man said. Honi asked whether he expected to live seventy years. The man said that as his fathers had planted for him, he was planting for his children. Honi sat down to eat, and fell asleep, and a ledge of rock grew up around him and hid him, and he slept for seventy years. When he woke a man was gathering carobs from the tree. Are you the one who planted it? No, said the man, I am his grandson. Honi went to the house of study and heard the scholars saying that the law was as clear as it had been in the days of Honi the Circle-Drawer. I am he, he said. No one believed him. He prayed for mercy, and died. Hence the saying: either companionship or death.

He was called the Circle-Drawer because once, in a drought, he drew a circle in the dust and stood in it and said he would not step out until it rained.

Epimenides of Crete, according to Diogenes Laertius, was sent by his father to look for a sheep. He lay down in a cave at midday and slept for fifty-seven years. When he woke he went on looking for the sheep. He lived, the Cretans said, to two hundred and ninety-nine, and he is remembered by Aristotle for a peculiar talent: he did not prophesy the future, only the past.

Of the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus it is said that in the reign of the emperor Decius seven young men who would not sacrifice to the gods hid in a cave on the mountain above the city, and the entrance was sealed with stones, and they slept. When they woke they were hungry and sent one of them, Malchus, into the city for bread. He paid with a coin bearing the face of an emperor two hundred years dead. In the Golden Legend the sleep lasts three hundred and seventy-two years, though the author admits the arithmetic does not come out. In the Qur'an, where the sleepers have a dog that lies at the threshold with its forepaws stretched out, it lasts three hundred years, and nine more.

In the 1920s, archaeologists digging on the slope of Mount Pion at Ephesus found a church built over a cave, and the cave full of graves.

Washington Irving wrote *Rip Van Winkle* in Birmingham, England, in 1818, and published it under the name of a Dutch historian who did not exist. Rip drinks from a keg belonging to some silent men who are bowling in the mountains, sleeps twenty years, and comes down to find that his beard is a foot long, his gun has rusted, his dog is gone, and the king on the inn sign has become General Washington. The story was adapted from a German tale about a goatherd named Peter Klaus.

Folklorists call this story type 766. The Seven Sleepers lend it their name.

Along the roads that crossed Persia and Anatolia the caravanserais were built about a day's march apart, thirty or forty kilometers, so that a caravan could expect to sleep each night behind a wall. One gate, wide enough for a loaded camel. Inside, a courtyard; around it, arcades for the animals and bales; above, rooms for the people. The sultan who finished the Sultan Han near Aksaray, in 1229, had the Qur'an carved over its door. It is said that Shah Abbas built nine hundred and ninety-nine of them, not a thousand, because a thousand is too easy to remember.

In 2023 fifty-four caravanserais in Iran were placed on the World Heritage list. Most of them are empty.

The Japanese word *ma* is written with the sign for a gate, and inside the gate, the sun. In the older form of the character it was the moon. The same character is the second half of the word for time, the second half of the word for space, and the second half of the word for human being, *ningen*: a person, and the interval.

Zeami, who made the Noh theater what it is, wrote in his notes for actors that what the actor does not do is interesting.

In Rome, the earliest Christian graves say only *in pace*. Later, *dormit in pace*: sleeps in peace. Later still, a wish: *requiescat in pace*, may he rest. The verb is in the subjunctive. It is not a report.

The Hebrew stones carry five letters, which stand for a line from the first book of Samuel: may his soul be bound up in the bundle of life.

A seed of Judean date palm, found at Masada and about two thousand years old, was planted in 2005 and grew. Seeds of a small white campion, buried by squirrels in Siberian permafrost some thirty thousand years ago, were grown into plants in 2012. They flowered.

Dormice sleep for seven months of the year. The Romans kept them in jars of clay with little ledges inside, and fed them acorns and chestnuts in the dark, and ate them with honey.

The jars were called *gliraria*. Some have been dug up with the ledges still in them, spiraling up the inside wall, and nothing on the ledges.
