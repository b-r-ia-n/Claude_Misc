# The Sixth Hour

The Romans had a word for leisure, *otium*, and a word for business, *negotium*, which means "not-leisure." Work was named for the thing it interrupted.

The Greeks did the same. Business was *ascholia*, unleisure. Their word for leisure, *scholē*, became the English word *school*.

Aristotle wrote that we are busy in order to have leisure, as we make war in order to have peace.

Cicero reports that Scipio Africanus used to say he was never less idle than when he was at leisure. Cicero wrote this down during a leisure of his own, one he had not chosen: the state of the Republic had left him with nothing to do.

---

The Romans counted the hours of daylight from sunrise. The sixth hour, *sexta hora*, was noon. In Spanish it became *siesta*.

The Rule of Saint Benedict allows the monks, after the meal at the sixth hour, to rest on their beds in silence. A monk who would rather read may read, but to himself, so as not to disturb the others.

---

In the Greek translation of the Psalms, among the terrors the faithful need not fear is "the demon of noon."

In the fourth century, in the Egyptian desert, the monk Evagrius Ponticus gave this demon a name: *acedia*, listlessness, carelessness, the absence of care. He wrote that it attacks around the fourth hour and lays siege to the soul until the eighth. It makes the sun seem to stand still. It makes the day seem fifty hours long. It makes the monk look out the window, and then go out of his cell, and then study the sun to see how far it is from the ninth hour, when he may eat. It makes him look around to see whether one of the brothers is coming.

John Cassian carried acedia from Egypt to Gaul. Pope Gregory the Great folded it into sadness. Later it became sloth. Later still, a slow mammal of the South American canopy was named after the sin.

---

Augustine records that Seneca mocked the Jews for losing a seventh part of their lives to idleness.

The French Revolution abolished the seven-day week and replaced it with a ten-day *décade*. There was one day of rest in ten instead of one in seven. The calendar lasted twelve years.

In 1929 the Soviet Union introduced the *nepreryvka*, the uninterrupted week, five days long. Workers were sorted into groups, by some accounts each with its own color, and each group had a different day off, so that the factories never stopped. A husband and wife might never have the same day free. The experiment was abandoned in stages; the seven-day week came back in 1940.

---

In Cockaigne, according to a Middle English poem of the early fourteenth century, the houses are built of pastries and the geese fly about already roasted, crying "Geese, all hot!"

In Cockaigne, according to an Old French fabliau, the more a man sleeps the more he earns. A man who sleeps until noon is paid five and a half sous.

In Cockaigne, according to the Dutch, there is a mountain of porridge, and the only way in is to eat through it. In Bruegel's painting of 1567, one man has just come out the other side.

---

In the workshops of eighteenth-century England, the week began on Tuesday. Monday was Saint Monday, a holy day with no saint, kept by shoemakers, weavers, cutlers, printers, and anyone else paid by the piece rather than the hour. The work was made up later in the week, at night.

Benjamin Franklin, working as a printer in London in 1725, noted with satisfaction that he never made a Saint Monday, and that his master noticed.

In Germany it was called Blue Monday.

---

In 1817 Robert Owen proposed eight hours of labor, eight hours of recreation, eight hours of rest.

In 1856 the stonemasons of Melbourne laid down their tools and won the eight-hour day.

In 1878 an American song put it this way: eight hours for work, eight hours for rest, eight hours for what we will.

In 1880 Paul Lafargue, a doctor from Cuba who had married Karl Marx's daughter Laura, published *The Right to Be Lazy*, a refutation of the right to work proclaimed in 1848. He thought three hours a day would be enough. The rest of the day and night could be given over to idleness and feasting.

Lafargue had decided he would not live past seventy. In 1911, shortly before his seventieth birthday, he and Laura died together at their house outside Paris. Lenin spoke at the funeral.

In 1930 John Maynard Keynes predicted that his grandchildren would work fifteen hours a week. In 1932 Bertrand Russell, in an essay in praise of idleness, proposed four hours a day.

---

The *Daodejing* says that the Way does nothing, and yet nothing is left undone.

In the *Zhuangzi*, a carpenter passes a huge oak growing beside a village shrine and does not stop to look: the wood is worthless, he tells his apprentice; that is why the tree has lived so long. That night the oak comes to him in a dream. It has spent a long time, it says, trying to be useless, and now it has succeeded.

Huizi complains to Zhuangzi that he owns a great tree whose trunk is too knotted for a carpenter's line and whose branches are too crooked for a compass. Zhuangzi suggests that he plant it somewhere with nothing in it, and wander beside it doing nothing, and lie down underneath it and sleep.

---

In April 2021, a former factory worker named Luo Huazhong posted an essay online under the title "Lying Flat Is Justice." He had quit his job, cycled from Sichuan to Tibet, and found he could live on about two hundred yuan a month. The censors removed the post, and later the forums where people discussed it. That October, an article by Xi Jinping in the Party journal *Qiushi* warned against "lying flat."

In Japan there is a word, *inemuri*, "to be present while asleep." To fall asleep during a meeting is not necessarily a disgrace: it may be taken as a sign that one has been working very hard.

---

In 1859 Ivan Goncharov published *Oblomov*, a novel whose hero spends most of the first part trying to get out of bed. The same year the critic Nikolai Dobrolyubov published an essay asking what "Oblomovism" was. In 1922 Lenin complained that Russia had made three revolutions and the Oblomovs were still there.

---

On October 17, 1492, Columbus noted that the people of the islands slept in nets of cotton hung between posts. The Taíno called them *hamaca*.

---

In Oblomov's dream of the estate where he was a child, the whole house sleeps after the midday meal: the master and the mistress, the aunts, the visitors, the servants. Only the boy is awake. He walks out into the garden. The sun has not moved.
