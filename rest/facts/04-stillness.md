# Facts gathered for "The Fermata" (04-stillness)

Entries marked "used" appear in the essay; others were gathered and left out.

### Whole rest and half rest
- **Fact:** In standard notation the whole rest hangs below a staff line and the half rest sits on top of one; a common mnemonic is that the whole rest is "heavier." (used)
- **Source:** Standard music-theory texts, e.g. Gardner Read, *Music Notation* (1969)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, notation, rest, mnemonic

### Multi-measure rest
- **Fact:** A rest lasting several bars is written as a thick horizontal bar with the number of silent measures above it. (used)
- **Source:** Gardner Read, *Music Notation*; Elaine Gould, *Behind Bars* (2011)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, notation, rest, silence

### Fermata means bus stop
- **Fact:** In modern Italian *fermata* is the ordinary word for a stop, including a bus or tram stop. (used)
- **Source:** Any Italian dictionary, e.g. Treccani, s.v. "fermata"
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, italian, etymology, pause

### Fermata sign and meaning
- **Fact:** The fermata is a dot beneath an arc placed over a note or rest; it instructs the performer to hold it longer than its written value, for an unspecified duration at the performer's discretion. Jazz and pop musicians nickname it the "bird's eye." (used)
- **Source:** Grove Music Online, "Fermata"; Gould, *Behind Bars*
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, notation, pause, fermata

### Beethoven's Fifth fermatas
- **Fact:** The opening of Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 in C minor (1808) consists of two four-note figures; the fourth note of each is held under a fermata. (used)
- **Source:** Beethoven, Symphony No. 5, Op. 67, score, mm. 1-5
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, beethoven, fermata, symphony

### General pause (G.P.)
- **Fact:** In orchestral scores the abbreviation G.P. (*Generalpause*) marks a bar or beat in which every part rests simultaneously. (used)
- **Source:** Grove Music Online, "General pause"; Gould, *Behind Bars*
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** music, notation, silence, orchestra

### Haydn's "Farewell" Symphony
- **Fact:** In the final adagio of Haydn's Symphony No. 45 in F-sharp minor (1772), players stop one after another, snuff their candles and leave, until only two muted violins remain. The story is that Haydn did this to hint to Prince Esterházy that the musicians wanted to go home to their families. (used)
- **Source:** H. C. Robbins Landon, *Haydn: Chronicle and Works*, vol. 2; Georg August Griesinger, *Biographische Notizen über Joseph Haydn* (1810) for the anecdote
- **Status:** fact (the musical exit is in the score; the motive is anecdotal)
- **Tags:** music, haydn, symphony, departure, eighteenth-century

### Morse code spacing
- **Fact:** In International Morse code the gap between letters is three units of silence and the gap between words is seven units.
- **Source:** ITU-R Recommendation M.1677-1 (2009), "International Morse code"
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** telegraphy, silence, interval, code

### 4'33" premiere
- **Fact:** John Cage's 4'33" was first performed by David Tudor on August 29, 1952, at the Maverick Concert Hall near Woodstock, New York, a wooden hall open at the back to the woods. Tudor marked the three movements by closing and opening the keyboard lid. (used)
- **Source:** Kyle Gann, *No Such Thing as Silence: John Cage's 4'33"* (Yale, 2010); James Pritchett, *The Music of John Cage* (1993)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** cage, tudor, silence, 1952, premiere

### 4'33" movement lengths
- **Fact:** At the premiere the three movements lasted 30 seconds, 2 minutes 23 seconds, and 1 minute 40 seconds, totaling 4 minutes 33 seconds. Later versions of the score allow any durations. (used)
- **Source:** Gann, *No Such Thing as Silence*; the 1960 Peters "Tacet" edition of the score
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** cage, silence, duration, score

### Cage on what the audience heard
- **Fact:** Cage later said that the audience missed the point: during the first movement the wind could be heard in the trees, during the second rain began to patter on the roof, and during the third the people themselves made sounds as they talked or walked out. (used)
- **Source:** Cage, interview quoted in Richard Kostelanetz, *Conversing with Cage* (1988); Gann, *No Such Thing as Silence*
- **Status:** fact (Cage's own account)
- **Tags:** cage, silence, audience, weather

### Silent Prayer and Muzak
- **Fact:** In a 1948 lecture at Vassar, "A Composer's Confessions," Cage said he wanted to compose a piece of uninterrupted silence, three or four and a half minutes long, titled *Silent Prayer*, and sell it to the Muzak Co.
- **Source:** John Cage, "A Composer's Confessions" (1948), printed in *Musicworks* 52 (1992) and in *John Cage: Writer*, ed. Kostelanetz
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** cage, silence, muzak, 1948

### Anechoic chamber
- **Fact:** Cage said that in an anechoic chamber at Harvard (around 1951) he heard a high sound and a low sound, and that the engineer told him the high one was his nervous system and the low one his blood circulating. Later commentators note that the "nervous system" explanation is physiologically doubtful and the high sound was more likely tinnitus. (used)
- **Source:** Cage, *Silence* (1961), "Experimental Music"; Gann, *No Such Thing as Silence*, for the critique
- **Status:** disputed (Cage's account is well attested; the engineer's explanation is doubted)
- **Tags:** cage, silence, harvard, acoustics, body

### Tacet score
- **Fact:** The 1960 Edition Peters version of 4'33" gives each of the three movements the single word "TACET," the Latin instruction meaning "it is silent." (used)
- **Source:** John Cage, 4'33", Edition Peters 6777 (1960)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** cage, score, latin, silence

### Rauschenberg's White Paintings
- **Fact:** Cage credited Robert Rauschenberg's all-white canvases of 1951 with giving him the courage to write the silent piece.
- **Source:** Cage, "On Robert Rauschenberg, Artist, and His Work," in *Silence* (1961)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** cage, rauschenberg, painting, silence

### Newton's first law
- **Fact:** Newton's first law (Principia, 1687) states that every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless compelled to change that state by impressed forces; the Latin phrase is "in statu suo quiescendi vel movendi uniformiter in directum." (used)
- **Source:** Isaac Newton, *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687), Axiomata sive Leges Motus, Lex I; English from Andrew Motte's translation (1729)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** physics, newton, rest, inertia, latin

### Rest mass
- **Fact:** The mass a body has in the frame in which it is at rest is called its rest mass (or invariant mass); the photon has a rest mass of zero.
- **Source:** Standard physics textbooks, e.g. Taylor and Wheeler, *Spacetime Physics*
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** physics, relativity, rest, light

### Arctic ground squirrel supercooling
- **Fact:** Hibernating Arctic ground squirrels (*Urocitellus parryii*) let their core body temperature fall to about -2.9 °C, below the freezing point of water, without freezing; they rewarm periodically every two to three weeks.
- **Source:** Brian M. Barnes, "Freeze Avoidance in a Mammal: Body Temperatures Below 0 °C in an Arctic Hibernator," *Science* 244 (1989): 1593-95
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** hibernation, biology, arctic, cold

### Honi and the carob tree
- **Fact:** In the Babylonian Talmud, Honi HaMe'agel sees a man planting a carob tree that will not bear for seventy years; the man says he plants for his descendants as his ancestors planted for him. Honi falls asleep, a rock formation hides him, and he sleeps seventy years; waking, he finds the planter's grandson gathering carobs. (used)
- **Source:** Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 23a
- **Status:** legend (rabbinic aggadah)
- **Tags:** talmud, sleep, seventy, carob, legend

### Honi unrecognized
- **Fact:** Honi goes to the study house, hears the scholars say the law is as clear as in the days of Honi, declares himself, is not believed, prays for mercy and dies. Rava comments with the proverb "either companionship or death" (*o chavruta o mituta*). (used)
- **Source:** Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 23a
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** talmud, death, loneliness, proverb

### Honi in the Jerusalem Talmud
- **Fact:** The Jerusalem Talmud gives a different version in which Honi sleeps seventy years spanning the destruction of the First Temple and the building of the Second.
- **Source:** Jerusalem Talmud, Ta'anit 3:9 (3:10 in some editions)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** talmud, sleep, temple, variant

### Honi's circle
- **Fact:** Honi earned his name by drawing a circle in a drought, standing inside it, and vowing not to leave until God sent rain. The incident is also reported by Josephus, who calls him Onias and says he was stoned to death around 65 BCE. (used)
- **Source:** Mishnah Ta'anit 3:8; Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.2.1
- **Status:** legend (Josephus's report of the stoning is historical testimony; the rain story is legend)
- **Tags:** talmud, rain, circle, josephus

### Epimenides' sleep
- **Fact:** According to Diogenes Laertius, Epimenides of Crete was sent by his father to fetch a sheep, lay down in a cave at midday, and slept for fifty-seven years; on waking he continued looking for the sheep. (used)
- **Source:** Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 1.109-115
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** greece, crete, sleep, cave, legend

### Epimenides' age
- **Fact:** Diogenes Laertius reports that Epimenides lived to 157, or, according to the Cretans, 299. (used)
- **Source:** Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 1.111
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** greece, longevity, crete

### Epimenides prophesied the past
- **Fact:** Aristotle says that Epimenides did not divine the future but things past that were obscure. (used)
- **Source:** Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 3.17 (1418a)
- **Status:** fact (as a report by Aristotle)
- **Tags:** greece, prophecy, aristotle, time

### Epimenides and the Cretan liar
- **Fact:** The Epistle to Titus quotes "a prophet of their own" saying Cretans are always liars; the line is traditionally attributed to Epimenides, which produces the "Epimenides paradox."
- **Source:** Titus 1:12; attribution via Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 1.14
- **Status:** disputed (attribution traditional, not certain)
- **Tags:** paradox, crete, bible, logic

### Seven Sleepers legend
- **Fact:** Seven young Christians of Ephesus, refusing to sacrifice under the emperor Decius (r. 249-251), hid in a cave that was sealed; they slept and awoke in the reign of Theodosius II, roughly two centuries later. One, called Malchus in the Latin versions, went to buy bread with an obsolete coin and was taken for a treasure-finder. (used)
- **Source:** Jacob of Serugh, Syriac homily (early 6th c.); Gregory of Tours, *Passio septem dormientium* (6th c.); Jacobus de Voragine, *Legenda aurea* (c. 1260)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** ephesus, sleep, cave, martyrs, legend

### Golden Legend arithmetic
- **Fact:** The *Golden Legend* says the sleepers slept 372 years but notes that this cannot be reconciled with the dates of Decius and Theodosius, and that they may have slept only about 195 years. (used)
- **Source:** Jacobus de Voragine, *Legenda aurea*, "The Seven Sleepers"; trans. William Granger Ryan (1993)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** medieval, chronology, legend, ephesus

### Sleepers in the Qur'an
- **Fact:** Surah 18 (al-Kahf, "The Cave") tells of youths who slept in a cave with their dog stretched at the entrance; they remained there "three hundred years, and add nine." (used)
- **Source:** Qur'an 18:9-26, esp. 18:18 and 18:25
- **Status:** legend (as scripture narrative)
- **Tags:** quran, cave, dog, sleep, islam

### Seven Sleepers' feast day and weather lore
- **Fact:** The Western feast of the Seven Sleepers is July 27 (Siebenschläfertag in German); German folk belief holds that the weather on that day sets the pattern for the next seven weeks. The German name for the edible dormouse, Siebenschläfer, comes from the same association.
- **Source:** *Butler's Lives of the Saints*, July 27; German folklore handbooks (e.g. *Handwörterbuch des deutschen Aberglaubens*, "Siebenschläfer")
- **Status:** fact (the feast and the belief exist; the weather rule is folk belief)
- **Tags:** calendar, weather, folklore, germany, dormouse

### Grotto of the Seven Sleepers
- **Fact:** Austrian excavations in 1927-28 on the slope of Mount Pion (Panayır Dağı) at Ephesus uncovered a church built over a cave complex with hundreds of graves, venerated since late antiquity as the Sleepers' cave. (used)
- **Source:** Franz Miltner, *Das Cömeterium der Sieben Schläfer* (Forschungen in Ephesos IV/2, 1937)
- **Status:** fact (the site is real; its identification is traditional)
- **Tags:** archaeology, ephesus, turkey, cave, church

### Tale type 766
- **Fact:** In the Aarne-Thompson-Uther index of folktale types, the long-sleep story is type ATU 766, "The Seven Sleepers." (used)
- **Source:** Hans-Jörg Uther, *The Types of International Folktales* (2004), no. 766
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** folklore, index, sleep, classification

### Rip Van Winkle composition
- **Fact:** Washington Irving wrote "Rip Van Winkle" in 1818 while living with his sister's family in Birmingham, England; it appeared in 1819 in the first installment of *The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.*, presented as a posthumous paper of the fictional historian Diedrich Knickerbocker. (used)
- **Source:** Stanley T. Williams, *The Life of Washington Irving* (1935); Irving, *The Sketch Book* (1819-20)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** irving, america, sleep, fiction, 1819

### Rip's twenty years
- **Fact:** Rip drinks from a keg belonging to silent, old-fashioned men playing ninepins in the Catskills, sleeps twenty years through the Revolution, and returns with a foot-long beard and a rusted gun to find the portrait of King George on the inn sign repainted as George Washington. (used)
- **Source:** Irving, "Rip Van Winkle," *The Sketch Book* (1819)
- **Status:** fact (plot of a fictional work)
- **Tags:** irving, catskills, revolution, sleep

### Peter Klaus source
- **Fact:** Irving's story closely follows the German tale of Peter Klaus, a goatherd who sleeps twenty years in the Kyffhäuser, printed in Otmar's (J. C. C. Nachtigal's) *Volcks-Sagen* (1800). (used)
- **Source:** Nachtigal, *Volcks-Sagen* (Bremen, 1800); Henry A. Pochmann, "Irving's German Sources in The Sketch Book," *Studies in Philology* 27 (1930)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** irving, germany, source, folktale

### Caravanserai spacing
- **Fact:** Caravanserais on the Persian and Anatolian routes were typically built a day's journey apart, about 30 to 40 km, with a single gate, a central courtyard, arcades for animals and goods, and rooms for travelers. (used)
- **Source:** UNESCO nomination file, "The Persian Caravanserai" (2023); Kurt Erdmann, *Das anatolische Karavansaray des 13. Jahrhunderts* (1961)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** silk-road, architecture, travel, persia, anatolia

### Sultan Han, Aksaray
- **Fact:** The Sultan Han on the Konya-Aksaray road, among the largest Seljuk caravanserais in Anatolia, was built under Sultan Alaeddin Keykubad I and dated by inscription to 1229 (AH 626). (used)
- **Source:** Erdmann, *Das anatolische Karavansaray*; building inscription
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** seljuk, turkey, architecture, 1229

### Shah Abbas's 999 caravanserais
- **Fact:** Popular tradition credits Shah Abbas I (r. 1588-1629) with building 999 caravanserais, with the explanation that a round thousand would have sounded less impressive or too easy to remember. (used)
- **Source:** Widely repeated in Iranian popular history and tourism literature; no primary source
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** persia, safavid, number, legend

### Persian caravanserais UNESCO
- **Fact:** In September 2023 UNESCO inscribed "The Persian Caravanserai," a serial property of 54 caravanserais across Iran, on the World Heritage List. (used)
- **Source:** UNESCO World Heritage Centre, decision 45 COM 8B.29 (2023)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** unesco, iran, heritage, 2023

### The character 間 (ma)
- **Fact:** The Japanese word *ma* (interval, gap, pause) is written 間, composed of the gate radical 門 with 日 (sun) inside; the older form 閒 has 月 (moon) inside the gate. (used)
- **Source:** Standard kanji etymologies, e.g. Shirakawa Shizuka, *Jōyō jikai*; Kenneth Henshall, *A Guide to Remembering Japanese Characters*
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** japan, kanji, interval, etymology

### Ma in compound words
- **Fact:** The same character is the second element of *jikan* (時間, time), *kūkan* (空間, space), and *ningen* (人間, human being). (used)
- **Source:** Any Japanese dictionary
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** japan, language, time, space

### Isozaki's Ma exhibition
- **Fact:** The architect Arata Isozaki curated the exhibition "Ma: Space-Time in Japan" for the Festival d'Automne in Paris in 1978; it traveled to the Cooper-Hewitt Museum in New York in 1979.
- **Source:** *Ma: Space-Time in Japan*, exhibition catalogue (Cooper-Hewitt, 1979)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** japan, architecture, exhibition, isozaki

### Zeami on not doing
- **Fact:** Zeami Motokiyo (c. 1363-c. 1443) wrote that "what the actor does not do is interesting" (*senu tokoro ga omoshiroki*), referring to the charged intervals between actions in Noh. (used)
- **Source:** Zeami, *Kakyō* (Mirror of the Flower, 1424); trans. in Rimer and Yamazaki, *On the Art of the Nō Drama* (1984)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** japan, noh, theater, pause, zeami

### Early Christian epitaphs
- **Fact:** The earliest Christian grave inscriptions in the Roman catacombs commonly use "in pace" alone or "dormit in pace" (sleeps in peace); the subjunctive formula "requiescat in pace" became standard on tombstones from about the eighth century. (used)
- **Source:** Orazio Marucchi, *Christian Epigraphy* (1912); *Catholic Encyclopedia*, "Christian Epitaphs"
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** latin, epitaph, rome, death, subjunctive

### Requiescat and the Psalms
- **Fact:** The phrase echoes the Vulgate Psalm 4:9 ("in pace in idipsum dormiam et requiescam") and the introit of the Requiem Mass, "Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine," from 4 Esdras 2:34-35.
- **Source:** Vulgate, Psalm 4:9; Roman Missal, Missa pro defunctis
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** latin, liturgy, psalms, requiem

### Hebrew tombstone abbreviation
- **Fact:** Jewish gravestones commonly end with the five-letter abbreviation תנצב"ה, standing for "may his/her soul be bound up in the bond of life," from 1 Samuel 25:29. (used)
- **Source:** 1 Samuel 25:29; *Encyclopaedia Judaica*, "Tombstones"
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** hebrew, epitaph, death, bible

### Judean date palm seed
- **Fact:** A date seed recovered from Herod's palace at Masada, radiocarbon-dated to roughly 2,000 years old, was germinated in 2005 and grew into a tree nicknamed Methuselah. (used)
- **Source:** Sarah Sallon et al., "Germination, Genetics, and Growth of an Ancient Date Seed," *Science* 320 (2008): 1464
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** seed, dormancy, israel, botany

### Silene stenophylla from permafrost
- **Fact:** Fruit tissue of the narrow-leafed campion, buried by ground squirrels in Siberian permafrost and dated to about 32,000 years, was regenerated into flowering plants in 2012. (used)
- **Source:** Svetlana Yashina et al., "Regeneration of whole fertile plants from 30,000-y-old fruit tissue buried in Siberian permafrost," *PNAS* 109 (2012): 4008-13
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** seed, permafrost, siberia, botany, dormancy

### Ancient lotus seed
- **Fact:** A sacred lotus seed from a dry lakebed in northeastern China, radiocarbon-dated to about 1,300 years old, was germinated in the 1990s.
- **Source:** Jane Shen-Miller et al., "Exceptional seed longevity and robust growth: ancient sacred lotus from China," *American Journal of Botany* 82 (1995): 1367-80
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** seed, lotus, china, dormancy

### Edible dormouse hibernation
- **Fact:** The edible dormouse (*Glis glis*) hibernates for roughly seven months a year, and in some years individuals have been recorded staying underground for more than eleven months. (used)
- **Source:** Claudia Bieber and Thomas Ruf, "Summer dormancy in edible dormice," *Journal of Mammalogy* 90 (2009): 1016-23; Hoelzl et al., *Journal of Comparative Physiology B* (2015)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** hibernation, dormouse, biology, sleep

### Roman gliraria
- **Fact:** Romans fattened dormice for the table in clay jars (gliraria) with internal ledges or spiral ramps and air holes, feeding them acorns, walnuts and chestnuts in the dark; Varro describes the practice and examples of the jars survive from Pompeii. (used)
- **Source:** Varro, *De re rustica* 3.15; Petronius, *Satyricon* 31 (dormice with honey and poppy seed); finds in the Naples archaeological museum
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** rome, dormouse, food, archaeology

### Caesura
- **Fact:** In prosody a caesura is a pause within a line of verse, marked in scansion with a double bar ||.
- **Source:** Standard handbooks, e.g. *Princeton Encyclopedia of Poetry and Poetics*, "Caesura"
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** poetry, pause, notation, prosody

### Barbarossa in the Kyffhäuser
- **Fact:** Legend says Emperor Frederick Barbarossa sleeps beneath the Kyffhäuser mountain at a stone table, his red beard grown through it, and will wake when the ravens stop circling; the "Peter Klaus" tale is set on the same mountain.
- **Source:** Grimm, *Deutsche Sagen* (1816), no. 23; Friedrich Rückert's poem "Barbarossa" (1817)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** germany, sleeping-king, mountain, legend

### Shmita: the land rests
- **Fact:** Leviticus prescribes that every seventh year the land of Israel lie fallow, "a sabbath of rest unto the land."
- **Source:** Leviticus 25:1-7
- **Status:** fact (as a scriptural commandment)
- **Tags:** bible, sabbath, agriculture, rest
