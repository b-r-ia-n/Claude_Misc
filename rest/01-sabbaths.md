# Forty Less One

In the beginning there were six days of work, and then God stopped.

The Hebrew verb is *shavat*: to cease, to desist. Exodus adds that on the seventh day he rested *va-yinafash*, a word that can be read as "and caught his breath."

The Qur'an, describing the same six days, adds that no weariness touched Him.

In the older Mesopotamian poem of Atrahasis, the gods are tired of digging canals. Humans are made to do the digging so that the gods can rest. The humans multiply, and their noise keeps the god Enlil awake. He sends plague, then drought, then a flood.

In Babylon the fifteenth day of the month, the full moon, was called *shapattu*, the day of the quieting of the heart.

A Babylonian hemerology, copied for the library of Ashurbanipal, marks the seventh, fourteenth, nineteenth, twenty-first and twenty-eighth days of certain months as evil days. On them the shepherd of the great peoples is not to eat meat cooked over coals. He is not to change his garment or put on white. He is not to ride in his chariot, or speak as a ruler. The seer is not to give an oracle. The physician is not to lay his hand on the sick.

The nineteenth day was the forty-ninth day counted from the start of the month before. Seven sevens.

In the wilderness, according to Exodus, manna fell for six days and not on the seventh. On the sixth day a double portion fell. Manna kept overnight bred worms and stank, except on the night before the Sabbath, when it kept.

You shall kindle no fire in your dwellings on the Sabbath day.

A man was found gathering sticks on the Sabbath. The congregation held him until it could be determined what should be done with him.

The Mishnah lists the primary categories of work forbidden on the Sabbath and counts them as "forty less one." They are: sowing, plowing, reaping, binding sheaves, threshing, winnowing, sorting, grinding, sifting, kneading, baking. Shearing wool, bleaching it, combing it, dyeing it, spinning, warping, making two loops, weaving two threads, separating two threads, tying a knot, untying a knot, sewing two stitches, tearing in order to sew two stitches. Trapping a deer, slaughtering it, flaying it, salting it, curing its hide, scraping it, cutting it up. Writing two letters, erasing in order to write two letters. Building, demolishing. Extinguishing a fire, kindling a fire. Striking the final hammer blow. Carrying from one domain into another.

The Talmud derives the list from the labors used in building the Tabernacle. Rest is defined by the shape of a single construction project.

To write one letter is not to write.

Carrying is permitted within a private domain. An eruv makes a neighborhood into one private domain, legally a single shared courtyard. In many cities it is a wire strung along the tops of utility poles, inspected each week before the Sabbath. Most of the people who walk beneath it do not know it is there.

The Talmud says the eruv was instituted by King Solomon, and that a heavenly voice approved. This is a tradition.

On the Sabbath one may walk two thousand cubits beyond the edge of town.

Rabbi Akiva, asked by the Roman governor Turnus Rufus what made one day different from another, is said to have offered three proofs: the river Sambation, which rests on the Sabbath; the necromancer's ghost, which will not rise on the Sabbath; and the grave of Turnus Rufus's own father, which does not smoke on the Sabbath.

Pliny the Elder reports a stream in Judaea that dries up every Sabbath. Josephus reports a river in Syria that is dry for six days and flows on the seventh. The river rests either way.

Seneca, as quoted by Augustine, said the Jews lose a seventh part of their lives to idleness. Tacitus added that they gave every seventh year to sloth as well.

The seventh year is the *shmita*. The land is left unsown and its produce is ownerless. At its close, debts are released. The sage Hillel, seeing that people stopped lending as the seventh year approached, devised the *prosbul*, which transfers a loan to the court so that it survives the release.

According to Leviticus, if the people did not let the land rest, they would be scattered, and the land would enjoy its sabbaths while it lay desolate.

After seven sevens of years comes the Jubilee, in which land returns to its original families and slaves go free. "Proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof" is from the Jubilee passage. It is cast on the Liberty Bell, which is cracked.

In 321, Constantine ordered that on the venerable day of the Sun magistrates and city people rest and workshops close. Farmers could keep working, since the right day for sowing grain or planting vines might otherwise be lost.

In 1781 the Reverend Samuel Peters published a list of the "blue laws" of colonial Connecticut, among them that no woman should kiss her child on the Sabbath. Historians consider much of his list invented. It is sometimes said that the laws were printed on blue paper. There is no evidence for this.

In Bergen County, New Jersey, near one of the largest concentrations of shopping malls in the United States, most stores still close on Sunday.

In 1961 the Supreme Court held that Maryland's Sunday closing laws had become secular. The day of rest no longer needed a god.

Benedict's Rule assigns monks seven daytime offices and one at night: "Seven times a day I have praised you," and "At midnight I arose." Monks sleep clothed and belted, but not with their knives, so they do not cut themselves while asleep. A lamp burns in the dormitory until morning. After Compline no one is to speak.

The Rule also says idleness is the enemy of the soul. On Sundays the monks are to read. A monk too negligent or lazy to read is to be given work.

On October 1, 1929, the Soviet Union began the *nepreryvka*, the continuous week: four days of work and one off, with each worker assigned a color, and each color a different day of rest. The factories would never stop. Husbands and wives with different colors did not see each other. In 1931 a six-day week replaced it, with a common day off. In 1940 the seven-day week returned.

Vishnu sleeps on the coils of the serpent Shesha, whose other name is Ananta, the endless, floating on the ocean between the dissolution of one world and the making of the next. From his navel grows a lotus, and on the lotus sits Brahma, who will make the world again.

Every year, it is said, Vishnu also sleeps for four months, from the eleventh day of the bright half of Ashadha to the eleventh of Kartika. This is Chaturmas, the rainy season. Weddings are postponed. Jain monks stop traveling and stay in one place, so as not to crush the small creatures that come out with the rains. On the day he wakes, in many households, a tulsi plant is married to him.

In Bali, on the day after the dark moon of the ninth month, there is Nyepi. The night before, villages carry monstrous effigies, *ogoh-ogoh*, through the streets, then burn them. The next day there is no fire and no light, no work, no travel, no entertainment. The demons, returning, are supposed to find the island empty and pass on.

The airport closes for twenty-four hours. Since 2018 the mobile internet has also been switched off. Village wardens walk the empty roads to make sure no one else is walking them.

That night, from the beaches, the only lights are offshore: the anchor lights of ships, which are not observing anything, and the stars.
