# The Sleeping Ones

The bottlenose dolphin sleeps one half of its brain at a time. The left hemisphere rests while the right keeps watch, then they trade. The eye opposite the waking half stays open. A dolphin can go on like this for weeks, never entirely asleep and never entirely awake, swimming slowly in circles, surfacing to breathe.

Mallard ducks sleeping in a row do the same. In an experiment in 1999, the ducks at the two ends of the row kept the outer eye open, the eye that faced the world. The ducks in the middle closed both.

---

According to Pliny, the hare sleeps with its eyes open. According to the *Physiologus*, so does the lion, and when the lion sleeps with its eyes open it signifies the Lord, whose body slept in the tomb while his divinity kept watch.

---

In 2016 researchers glued small recording devices to the heads of great frigatebirds on the Galápagos and let them go. The birds flew out over the Pacific for up to ten days without landing. They slept, it turned out, in the air: in bouts of about twelve seconds, usually while circling upward on a rising current, sometimes with one half of the brain, sometimes with both. In flight they slept about forty-two minutes a day. Back on land, the same birds slept more than twelve hours.

The common swift leaves Europe in August and may not touch the ground again until it returns to breed. Some individuals tracked by Swedish researchers stayed aloft for ten months. They eat in the air, drink in the air, and, it is supposed, sleep in the air, though no one has yet recorded it.

---

Aristotle believed that swallows did not leave in winter but hid in holes, featherless. Olaus Magnus, Archbishop of Uppsala, wrote in 1555 that in the north the swallows gather in clusters, beak to beak and wing to wing, and sink into the reeds at the bottom of lakes, where fishermen sometimes pull them up in their nets, frozen; if carried to a warm room they revive, fly about briefly, and die. Samuel Johnson told Boswell he believed it. In 1703 a pamphlet attributed to the English clergyman Charles Morton proposed instead that the birds fly to the Moon, a journey of about two months, which they spend asleep.

---

There is one bird that does sleep through the winter. In December 1946, in the Chuckwalla Mountains of California, the naturalist Edmund Jaeger found a common poorwill tucked into a crevice in a canyon wall. Its body was cold, its breathing undetectable. He picked it up, put it back, and returned to find it there for several winters in a row. It is said that the Hopi already had a name for the bird, *hölchko*, "the sleeping one."

---

Hibernation is not sleep. The arctic ground squirrel lets its body fall to nearly three degrees below the freezing point of water and stays there for weeks. Every two or three weeks, at enormous metabolic cost, it rewarms itself to normal temperature for less than a day, then cools again. One explanation, still debated, is that it must wake in order to sleep.

The wood frog of Alaska freezes. Ice forms in its body cavity and under its skin; its heart stops; it does not breathe. In spring it thaws from the inside out, and the heart starts again on its own.

According to Pliny, the bear in its den sleeps so deeply for the first fourteen days that not even wounds will wake it, and in that time grows wonderfully fat on sleep alone. The Romans kept dormice in earthenware jars, the *glirarium*, and fattened them in the dark for the table.

---

Caesar writes that in the Hercynian forest there is an animal like a goat, called the *alces*, whose legs have no joints. It cannot lie down, and so sleeps leaning against a tree. Hunters find the trees, cut them nearly through, and wait. Pliny tells the same of the *achlis*. The medieval bestiaries tell it of the elephant, which, when its tree falls, lies on its back and cries out until a small elephant comes to lift it.

The real elephant, wild in Botswana, sleeps about two hours a night, often standing. Two matriarchs fitted with monitors once went forty-six hours without sleeping at all.

---

The giraffe sleeps in bouts of a few minutes. For its deepest sleep it folds its legs beneath it and curls its long neck backward to rest its head on its own haunch.

The northern elephant seal, at sea for months, sleeps about two hours a day in short naps taken during dives. Hundreds of feet down, it falls asleep, rolls over, and drifts in a slow spiral toward the bottom, turning like a leaf.

The chinstrap penguin, guarding its nest, sleeps in naps of about four seconds, more than ten thousand times a day.

The sloth was long said to sleep sixteen hours a day, based on sloths in zoos. In 2008, three-toed sloths were monitored in the rainforest of Panama. They slept nine and a half.

The little brown bat sleeps nearly twenty hours. The koala sleeps up to twenty-two. The walrus, in captivity, has been observed swimming and resting without sleep for more than three days.

---

Sea otters sleep floating on their backs. To keep from drifting apart they wrap themselves in kelp, or hold one another by the paw.

---

In the evening the parrotfish secretes a cocoon of mucus from glands near its gills and sleeps inside it. Certain wrasses bury themselves in the sand. Zebrafish, sleeping, sink a little and hang motionless near the bottom.

The octopus, asleep, goes pale, and every so often passes through a minute of twitching and flickering color, which some have called dreaming.

Some sharks must swim to breathe. It has been suggested that they swim in their sleep.

---

A bee that sleeps lets its antennae droop. Honeybees deprived of sleep perform their waggle dances less precisely, and their sisters fly in the wrong direction. Some male bees, having no hive, sleep alone, clinging to a stem or a flower by their jaws.

Fruit flies sleep, and are kept awake by caffeine. The nematode worm sleeps at each of its molts.

In 2017 researchers at Caltech showed that the upside-down jellyfish *Cassiopea*, which has no brain, sleeps. At night it pulses more slowly and is slower to notice when its perch is taken away. Kept awake by small jets of water, it is sluggish the following day.

---

Theophrastus records that Androsthenes, an admiral of Alexander, saw on the island of Tylos a tree whose leaves closed at night and opened again with the sun. In 1729 Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan shut a mimosa in a dark cupboard. It went on opening and closing its leaves on schedule, without the sun.

In 1751 Linnaeus proposed a flower clock: a garden planted so that one could tell the hour by which flowers were open and which were closed. Among the species on his list was the meadow goatsbeard, which in England is called Jack-go-to-bed-at-noon. In 1755 one of his students defended a dissertation titled *Somnus plantarum*, "The Sleep of Plants."

Darwin, in *The Power of Movement in Plants*, called the night movements of leaves "nyctitropic," and guessed that the leaves folded to keep from losing heat to the open sky.

---

In 2016, scientists in Finland and Austria scanned birch trees with lasers through the course of a still night. Toward midnight the branches had lowered by as much as ten centimeters. Before sunrise they began to lift.
