# Facts: Sleeping Animals and Plants

### Dolphin unihemispheric sleep
- **Fact:** Bottlenose dolphins sleep one brain hemisphere at a time, keeping the eye opposite the waking hemisphere open, and can stay continuously vigilant this way for days to weeks.
- **Source:** Mukhametov and colleagues' EEG studies of cetaceans (1970s-80s); vigilance duration from Branstetter et al., PLoS ONE 2012 (approximate)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** dolphins, unihemispheric, marine-mammals, science

### Ducks at the end of the row
- **Fact:** Mallards sleeping in a row: birds at the ends slept more with one eye open, the eye facing away from the group, than birds in the middle.
- **Source:** Rattenborg, Lima & Amlaner, Nature 1999
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** birds, unihemispheric, vigilance, science

### Hare sleeps with eyes open
- **Fact:** Pliny states that hares sleep with their eyes open.
- **Source:** Pliny, Natural History, book 11 (exact section not verified)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** hares, pliny, eyes, ancient

### Lion sleeps with eyes open
- **Fact:** The Physiologus and later bestiaries say the lion sleeps with its eyes open, an allegory of Christ whose body slept in the tomb while his divinity watched.
- **Source:** Physiologus (Greek, c. 2nd-4th century CE); medieval Latin bestiaries
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** lions, bestiary, allegory, medieval

### Frigatebirds sleep in flight
- **Fact:** Great frigatebirds flying up to ten days over the Pacific from the Galápagos slept in flight in bouts of about 12 seconds, mostly while circling on updrafts, using one or both hemispheres; about 42 minutes per day in flight versus over 12 hours on land.
- **Source:** Rattenborg et al., Nature Communications 2016
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** birds, flight, unihemispheric, science

### Swifts aloft for ten months
- **Fact:** Common swifts tracked with data loggers stayed airborne for up to about ten months of their nonbreeding period; sleep in flight is presumed but has not been directly recorded.
- **Source:** Hedenström et al., Current Biology 2016
- **Status:** fact (sleep on the wing: disputed/unconfirmed)
- **Tags:** birds, swifts, flight, migration

### Aristotle on hibernating swallows
- **Fact:** Aristotle reported that swallows and other birds hide in holes in winter and are found featherless.
- **Source:** Aristotle, History of Animals, book 8
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** birds, swallows, hibernation, ancient

### Olaus Magnus on swallows under the ice
- **Fact:** Olaus Magnus wrote that northern swallows cluster mouth to mouth and wing to wing and sink into lakes for winter; fishermen net them up, and if warmed they revive briefly and die.
- **Source:** Olaus Magnus, Historia de gentibus septentrionalibus, 1555
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** birds, swallows, hibernation, renaissance

### Samuel Johnson believed it
- **Fact:** Samuel Johnson told Boswell that swallows gather in a mass and throw themselves under water to winter in rivers.
- **Source:** Boswell, Life of Johnson (1791)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** birds, swallows, johnson, eighteenth-century

### Birds migrating to the Moon
- **Fact:** A pamphlet usually attributed to Charles Morton, published 1703, argued that migratory birds fly to the Moon, a trip of about two months that they pass largely in a sleeplike state.
- **Source:** "An Essay Towards the Probable Solution of this Question: Whence come the Stork and the Turtle..." (1703), attributed to Charles Morton; see Harrison, Isis 1954 (attribution approximate)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** birds, migration, moon, early-modern

### The hibernating poorwill
- **Fact:** In winter 1946-47 Edmund Jaeger found a torpid common poorwill in a rock crevice in the Chuckwalla Mountains, California, and found it there in following winters; it is the first bird documented to hibernate.
- **Source:** Jaeger, The Condor 1948 and 1949
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** birds, hibernation, torpor, california

### Hopi name for the poorwill
- **Fact:** The Hopi are said to call the poorwill hölchko, "the sleeping one."
- **Source:** reported by Jaeger (Condor 1948/49) and widely repeated; not independently verified
- **Status:** disputed
- **Tags:** birds, hopi, names, hibernation

### Arctic ground squirrel supercooling
- **Fact:** Hibernating arctic ground squirrels lower body temperature to about -2.9 C without freezing.
- **Source:** Barnes, Science 1989
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** mammals, hibernation, cold, science

### Waking in order to sleep
- **Fact:** Hibernators periodically rewarm to normal temperature (interbout arousals) for less than a day; one hypothesis is that they do so to sleep, since torpor accumulates a sleep debt.
- **Source:** Daan, Barnes & Strijkstra, Neuroscience Letters 1991; Trachsel et al., 1991
- **Status:** disputed
- **Tags:** hibernation, arousal, sleep-debt, science

### Frozen wood frogs
- **Fact:** Wood frogs survive freezing of a large share of their body water; heartbeat and breathing stop, glucose acts as a cryoprotectant, and they revive on thawing. Alaskan populations survive particularly long, deep freezes.
- **Source:** Storey & Storey (various, 1980s onward); Larson et al., Journal of Experimental Biology 2014 (Alaska)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** amphibians, freezing, hibernation, science

### Pliny on bears
- **Fact:** Pliny says bears in their dens sleep so soundly for the first fourteen days that even wounds will not rouse them, and grow remarkably fat from sleep.
- **Source:** Pliny, Natural History 8.54 (section number approximate)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** bears, pliny, hibernation, ancient

### Fattened dormice
- **Fact:** Romans fattened edible dormice in earthenware jars (glirarium) for eating; Pliny and Varro mention dormice and their winter sleep.
- **Source:** Varro, De re rustica 3.15; Pliny, Natural History book 8
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** dormice, rome, food, hibernation

### Jointless elk leaning on trees
- **Fact:** Caesar describes the alces of the Hercynian forest as having legs without joints, so it sleeps leaning against trees, which hunters undercut so the animal topples.
- **Source:** Caesar, Gallic War 6.27
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** elk, caesar, legs, ancient

### The achlis
- **Fact:** Pliny tells a similar story of the achlis, a northern animal without leg joints that sleeps leaning on a tree.
- **Source:** Pliny, Natural History 8.16 (section approximate)
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** pliny, achlis, legs, ancient

### Bestiary elephant
- **Fact:** Medieval bestiaries say the elephant has no knee joints and sleeps against a tree; hunters saw the tree, the elephant falls and cries out, and in the end a small elephant lifts it (allegory of Christ raising Adam).
- **Source:** medieval Latin bestiaries, e.g. Aberdeen Bestiary; derived from Physiologus
- **Status:** legend
- **Tags:** elephants, bestiary, allegory, medieval

### Wild elephants sleep two hours
- **Fact:** Two wild African elephant matriarchs in Botswana slept about two hours a day, often standing, and sometimes went about 46 hours without sleep.
- **Source:** Gravett et al., PLoS ONE 2017
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** elephants, mammals, short-sleep, science

### Giraffe sleep posture
- **Fact:** Giraffes sleep in short bouts; in paradoxical (REM) sleep they lie down and curl the neck back to rest the head on the haunch. Captive giraffes total roughly 4.6 hours of sleep per day.
- **Source:** Tobler & Schwierin, Journal of Sleep Research 1996 (captive)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** giraffes, mammals, posture, science

### Elephant seal sleep spirals
- **Fact:** Northern elephant seals at sea sleep about two hours a day, during deep dives, sometimes rolling over and drifting downward in a "sleep spiral."
- **Source:** Kendall-Bar et al., Science 2023
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** seals, marine-mammals, diving, science

### Penguin microsleeps
- **Fact:** Nesting chinstrap penguins sleep in microsleeps of about four seconds, over ten thousand times a day, adding up to around eleven hours.
- **Source:** Libourel et al., Science 2023
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** birds, penguins, microsleep, science

### Wild sloths sleep less
- **Fact:** Captive sloths were recorded sleeping about 16 hours; wild brown-throated three-toed sloths in Panama slept about 9.6 hours.
- **Source:** Rattenborg et al., Biology Letters 2008
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** sloths, mammals, captivity, science

### Long sleepers
- **Fact:** The little brown bat sleeps about 20 hours a day; koalas are often reported to sleep or rest 18-22 hours.
- **Source:** bat figure from Siegel's comparative compilations (e.g. Siegel, Nature 2005); koala figure widely reported, primary source unknown
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** bats, koalas, mammals, long-sleep

### Walrus without sleep
- **Fact:** Captive walruses were observed going up to about 84 hours without sleep while swimming and resting.
- **Source:** Pryaslova, Lyamin et al., c. 2009 (citation details uncertain)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** walrus, marine-mammals, short-sleep, science

### Sea otters holding paws
- **Fact:** Sea otters sleep floating on their backs, often anchored in kelp; resting otters sometimes hold paws, and they rest together in groups called rafts.
- **Source:** general natural history; paw-holding mostly documented in captivity and popular reports, primary source unknown
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** otters, marine-mammals, kelp, social

### Parrotfish mucus cocoon
- **Fact:** Many parrotfish secrete a mucus cocoon from glands near the gills at night and rest inside it; it may shield them from parasites such as gnathiid isopods.
- **Source:** Grutter et al., Biology Letters 2011
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** fish, reefs, cocoon, science

### Wrasses in the sand
- **Fact:** Many wrasses bury themselves in sand to rest at night.
- **Source:** general reef ichthyology; specific source unknown
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** fish, reefs, burrowing, night

### Zebrafish sleep
- **Fact:** Zebrafish show sleep: inactivity, a raised arousal threshold, and homeostatic rebound, often drooping and hovering near the bottom or surface.
- **Source:** Zhdanova et al., Brain Research 2001; Yokogawa et al., PLoS Biology 2007
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** fish, zebrafish, sleep, science

### Octopus active sleep
- **Fact:** Sleeping octopuses go pale and still, then periodically pass through about a minute of twitching and skin-pattern changes ("active sleep"), which some have compared to dreaming.
- **Source:** Medeiros et al., iScience 2021; Pophale et al., Nature 2023
- **Status:** fact (dreaming interpretation: disputed)
- **Tags:** octopus, cephalopods, dreaming, science

### Sharks swimming in sleep
- **Fact:** Some sharks are obligate ram ventilators and must keep swimming to breathe; whether and how they sleep while swimming is unresolved. A spinal-cord swimming pattern generator in the spiny dogfish has been cited as a possible mechanism.
- **Source:** unknown for the specific sleep claim; Kelly et al., Journal of Fish Biology 2019 reviews shark sleep
- **Status:** disputed
- **Tags:** sharks, fish, swimming, uncertain

### Honeybee sleep
- **Fact:** Sleeping honeybees let their antennae droop and show reduced muscle tone; this was described by Walter Kaiser in the 1980s.
- **Source:** Kaiser, Journal of Comparative Physiology A 1988
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** insects, bees, sleep, science

### Sleep-deprived bees dance badly
- **Fact:** Sleep-deprived honeybees performed less precise waggle dances, signaling direction less accurately.
- **Source:** Klein et al., PNAS 2010
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** insects, bees, communication, sleep-deprivation

### Male bees sleep holding on with their jaws
- **Fact:** Males of some solitary bees, having no nest, sleep clinging to plant stems or inside flowers, gripping with their mandibles.
- **Source:** general entomology; specific source unknown
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** insects, bees, mandibles, posture

### Fruit fly sleep and caffeine
- **Fact:** Drosophila have sleep with rebound after deprivation; caffeine reduces their sleep.
- **Source:** Hendricks et al., Neuron 2000; Shaw et al., Science 2000
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** insects, flies, caffeine, science

### Nematode lethargus
- **Fact:** C. elegans enters a sleeplike state called lethargus at each larval molt.
- **Source:** Raizen et al., Nature 2008
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** worms, nematodes, molting, science

### Brainless jellyfish sleep
- **Fact:** The upside-down jellyfish Cassiopea, which has no brain, pulses more slowly at night, responds more slowly to stimuli, and after sleep deprivation is less active the next day.
- **Source:** Nath et al., Current Biology 2017 (Caltech)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** jellyfish, cnidarians, brainless, science

### Hydra sleep
- **Fact:** Hydra, a brainless cnidarian, also shows sleeplike states.
- **Source:** Kanaya et al., Science Advances 2020
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** hydra, cnidarians, brainless, science

### Lizard sleep cycles
- **Fact:** Australian bearded dragons show alternating REM-like and slow-wave sleep states with a cycle of about 80 seconds.
- **Source:** Shein-Idelson et al., Science 2016
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** reptiles, lizards, rem, science

### Androsthenes's tree
- **Fact:** Theophrastus records that Androsthenes of Thasos, with Alexander's fleet, saw on Tylos (Bahrain) a tree whose leaves close at night and open at sunrise; usually taken as a tamarind or similar legume.
- **Source:** Theophrastus, Enquiry into Plants 4.7.8 (section approximate)
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, leaf-movement, ancient, alexander

### Mairan's mimosa
- **Fact:** In 1729 de Mairan kept a Mimosa in darkness and found its leaves still opened and closed on a daily rhythm, the first demonstration of an endogenous circadian rhythm.
- **Source:** de Mairan, report to the Académie Royale des Sciences, 1729
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, circadian, mimosa, eighteenth-century

### Linnaeus's flower clock
- **Fact:** Linnaeus proposed a horologium florae, a garden of flowers that open and close at known hours so the time could be read from them.
- **Source:** Linnaeus, Philosophia Botanica, 1751
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, linnaeus, clocks, eighteenth-century

### Jack-go-to-bed-at-noon
- **Fact:** Meadow goatsbeard (Tragopogon pratensis), whose flowers close around midday, is called Jack-go-to-bed-at-noon in English and appeared in Linnaeus's flower clock.
- **Source:** Linnaeus, Philosophia Botanica 1751; English folk name widely recorded
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, flowers, names, linnaeus

### The Sleep of Plants
- **Fact:** A dissertation titled Somnus plantarum (The Sleep of Plants), under Linnaeus as presider, was defended in 1755 by his student Petrus Bremer.
- **Source:** Linnaeus (praeses) and P. Bremer, Somnus plantarum, Uppsala 1755
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, linnaeus, nyctinasty, eighteenth-century

### Darwin on nyctitropism
- **Fact:** Darwin named the night movements of leaves "nyctitropic" and suggested they reduce heat loss by radiation to the night sky.
- **Source:** Charles Darwin (with Francis Darwin), The Power of Movement in Plants, 1880
- **Status:** fact (explanation: disputed)
- **Tags:** plants, darwin, nyctinasty, nineteenth-century

### Birch trees droop at night
- **Fact:** Terrestrial laser scans of birches in Finland and Austria showed branches drooping by up to about 10 cm at night and recovering around dawn.
- **Source:** Puttonen, Zlinszky et al., Frontiers in Plant Science 2016
- **Status:** fact
- **Tags:** plants, trees, lasers, science
